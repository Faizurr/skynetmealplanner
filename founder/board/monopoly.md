# Monopoly lens

## Idea A: AI phone caller
Score: 5 | Vote: FUND IF

### What is strong
- Clear "why now": off-the-shelf voice agents only recently made this cheap to build.
- The giants leave a small market open for now: Call for Me needs a Pixel 11, a US SIM and English; Apple's Hold Assist doesn't talk. Expats calling in a foreign language fall outside both.
- Expat communities are concentrated: a plausible path to the first 100 customers.

### What would kill it (top 3, most dangerous first)
1. **Platform risk.** Google and Apple own the dialer and are moving. Once Call for Me goes global and multilingual, a $12.99 app competes with a free OS feature.
2. **Copyable in a month.** The stack is Vapi or Retell plus Twilio. CallBud, Freebot and Xpat24 exist, and Pine has $25M. Nothing here is ten times better than a rival on the same platform.
3. **The other end of the line closes.** Offices can block bots (the unverified Berlin/Munich report); TCPA and recording-consent rules are unsettled for consumer-side agents.

### The questions I would ask the founder
- What share of calls finish without a takeover, and at what real cost?
- What know-how (phone-menu maps, scripts that work) will you own by month six that a Vapi copycat won't?
- Does this survive ten years, when businesses answer with their own agents or APIs?

### If FUND IF: the conditions
- Launch in one non-US country, one language pair, one kind of call, outside Google's footprint, and fast: Xpat24 already targets German calls for expats.
- A prototype that measures completion rate and cost per task before paid marketing.
- A written legal view on AI-voice disclosure and call recording there.

## Idea B: Official-letter explainer
Score: 3 | Vote: PASS

### What is strong
- Real, frequent pain; cents per letter to run.
- The archive and reminders create some switching cost.
- Long-tail search on specific letter types could be a free channel.

### What would kill it (top 3, most dangerous first)
1. **A free substitute in every pocket.** Pasting a photo into ChatGPT or Gemini already does the core job. Incremental, not tenfold.
2. **A crowded field with no leader.** Seven-plus look-alike apps mean a price war at $4.99. A copycat needs a weekend.
3. **Platform absorption.** Phone assistants are likely to summarise letters as a default feature.

### The questions I would ask the founder
- What does your app know about a country's letter types that a general model gets wrong?
- Why would someone who already pays for an assistant pay again?
- Who is liable when a wrong deadline on a court letter costs a user money?

### If FUND IF: the conditions
None. Revisit only with a country-specific knowledge base (letter type, deadline, correct action) that measurably beats general assistants.

## Idea C: Fair-price checker
Score: 6 | Vote: FUND IF

### What is strong
- **The only moat here a general AI can't copy from the open web:** real paid prices, line by line, city by city. Each check sharpens the next: a data network effect.
- Low platform risk: Apple, Google and the assistants have no local invoice data, only generic cost-guide ranges.
- A plausible secret: the true local price of a repair is knowable from paid invoices, and nobody publishes it line by line.

### What would kill it (top 3, most dangerous first)
1. **Cold start.** Without dense local data the verdict is no better than a free cost guide: a two-sided bootstrap problem in disguise.
2. **Someone else collects the data first.** QuoteScore already runs on submitted quotes, and RepairPal already offers car-repair estimates. The flywheel protects only whoever spins it first.
3. **Rare use.** Big repairs are rare, so retention is weak, and the cost of the first 100 customers against $4.99 a check is unknown.

### The questions I would ask the founder
- How many paid invoices does one job type in one metro need before the verdict is credibly local? (Open question.)
- How does each check capture the final price paid, not just the quote?
- What is the legal exposure when a verdict implies a named contractor overcharged?

### If FUND IF: the conditions
- One job type, one metro, with a seed set of real invoices before launch.
- Every check feeds the final price paid back into the database.
- Beat QuoteScore and a general assistant in a blind test on real quotes before paid marketing.
- Avoid car repair first; RepairPal is entrenched.

## My pick
**Idea C, on the conditions above.** The founder's "easy architecture" is exactly why A and B have no moat: what is easy to build is easy to copy, and a platform can ship it free. In C the defensible asset is the data, not the code, and it compounds where Apple, Google and the assistants can't follow. A is runner-up, but a timing play with no ten-year answer. Forget "1 billion people" as a start: own one high-ticket home job (say, water-heater or HVAC replacement) in one metro in the launch country. Then expand to nearby home trades in that metro, neighbouring metros, the whole country, dental and car repair, and finally other countries.
