# Product lens

## Idea A: AI that makes your phone calls
Score: 7 | Vote: FUND IF

### What is strong
- The only idea with a real "that's amazing" moment: type one line, put the phone down, the dreaded call gets made.
- One sentence already: "It makes the call you don't want to make." The result is concrete, with a transcript as proof.

### What would kill it (top 3, most dangerous first)
1. Seams the founder does not own. Each call crosses the app, a voice platform, Twilio, a phone menu and a stranger who may demand the account holder or hang up on a bot. If the one free call fails, that is the whole first impression.
2. Doing too much. Booking, cancelling, disputing, any language, live takeover, three platforms: each is its own product. Disputes and cancellations need identity checks; live takeover is useless if you don't speak the language.
3. The phone makers own the dialler. Google's Call for Me and Apple's Hold Assist sit inside the phone; an app is one hand-off further away.

### The questions I would ask the founder
- Which single task, in which country and language, first?
- Of 50 real calls of that task, how many finish with no human help? (Open question.)
- Median time from tap to outcome, and what fills it?
- What does the agent say when asked "are you a person?"
- Real cost per completed call? ($0.50 to $1.50 is an estimate.)

### If FUND IF: the conditions
- Version 1 does one job: booking or rescheduling appointments, in one country.
- Measure completion on real calls before charging; design the failure screen as carefully as the success screen.
- Settle AI-disclosure and recording-consent rules for the launch country first.

## Idea B: Official-letter explainer
Score: 5 | Vote: FUND IF

### What is strong
- The simplest experience: photograph a letter, learn whether you must act, by when, for how much. One screen.
- The founder can own almost the whole experience, and it brings real relief.

### What would kill it (top 3, most dangerous first)
1. No wow over the free option. Pasting the photo into a general AI assistant gives most of this. If the first 30 seconds are not plainly better, nobody switches.
2. One wrong deadline or amount on a tax or court letter ends trust for good. "Any letter, any country, any language" is too wide to get right.
3. The seam after the answer: the reply must leave the app by post, email or a portal it doesn't control. How often people get official letters is an open question.

### The questions I would ask the founder
- What exactly is on screen in the first ten seconds?
- How does the user check the deadline? Is the source line shown?
- Side by side with a general AI assistant, which do test users choose, and why?

### If FUND IF: the conditions
- One country and one group (newcomers) first.
- Every answer is one screen in one shape (act or not, deadline, amount, next step), with the source line highlighted.
- Cut reply drafting and archive search from version 1; keep the deadline reminder.

## Idea C: Fair-price checker
Score: 3 | Vote: PASS

### What is strong
- A clear moment of need (a big bill in hand) and a one-word answer (fair or padded).

### What would kill it (top 3, most dangerous first)
1. The verdict rests on price data the founder does not own. That unseen part is the whole product, and it is borrowed.
2. The product stops where the hard part starts: the conversation with the mechanic or plumber.
3. Four trades, four kinds of line items, each used rarely. No habit forms, and free tools set the bar.

### The questions I would ask the founder
- For one trade in one city, how often is the verdict right, checked against what? (Open question.)
- What happens when a user confronts a contractor and the app was wrong?

### If FUND IF: the conditions
None. PASS.

## My pick
Idea A, cut to one job. It is the only idea whose first use can make someone say "that's amazing, this is what I need". B's key moment is already free elsewhere; C rests on data it cannot control. A's danger is its seams, so start with the call that has the fewest: appointment booking, where nobody asks for an account PIN and the result is a calendar entry. Craft the failure path as carefully as the success path.

**The first five minutes.** She opens the link on her phone, types "Book a dentist cleaning next week, any morning" and picks the clinic. The app asks her date of birth, then shows "Calling now" with a live transcript in her language while the agent speaks the local one. She puts the phone down. A text arrives: "Booked: Tuesday 9:30. Bring your insurance card." One tap adds it to her calendar. If it fails: "They need you to call. Here is exactly what to say."

**Version 1.** In: a mobile-friendly web app, appointment booking and rescheduling, one country and its language, live transcript, outcome card, calendar add, one free call. Cut: cancellations, disputes, negotiation, live takeover, native apps, "any language".
